A context window is not a free feature. Every extra token has to be **stored** in the KV cache, **read** by every later decode step, and **attended to** during prefill. At 8K tokens those costs are tolerable. At 1M they decide the hardware, the latency SLO and the price per request.

The industry is attacking the problem with two independent levers:

1. **Store less per token**: GQA, MLA, KV quantization, and block compression.
2. **Read and compute less per query**: sparse attention, where an indexer chooses a small set of keys and the expensive attention runs only on those.

They are easy to confuse. **Sparse attention does not shrink the KV cache.** It cuts attention work and KV reads. Compression shrinks memory. A serious long-context design uses both.

## Two costs that scale differently

For one layer with context length *n*:

- **Prefill** compares every query with every key: *n²* score pairs. Attention becomes a large share of prompt processing at long context, even though the linear projections still matter.
- **Decode** has one query per step. It compares that query with *n* cached keys, so attention work is *O(n)*, and the bytes read from the KV cache also grow with *n*.
- **KV capacity** grows with *n* per request. The weights are paid once; the cache is paid per user.

That is why long context hurts different SLOs in different ways. Prefill length drives **TTFT**. KV size caps **concurrency**. Decode attention drives **ITL** and, at large batch, can turn a memory-bound step into a compute-bound one.

## Compress what you store

The first lever is the KV representation. The [attention](../attention/) and [KV cache](../kv-cache/) chapters derive the sizes; the long-context summary is:

| Scheme | What is cached per token | What it buys |
|---|---|---|
| MHA | One K and one V per query head | The baseline, and the largest cache |
| GQA | One K/V per group of query heads | 4–8× smaller cache at almost no quality cost |
| MQA | One K/V for all query heads | The smallest cache, with a quality risk |
| MLA | One low-rank latent per layer, plus a small RoPE key | Several times smaller than GQA, reconstructed by up-projections |
| FP8 / NVFP4 KV | The same entries at lower precision | Roughly 2× / 4× less memory, with accuracy and kernel caveats |

DeepSeek-V2 introduced **multi-head latent attention** (MLA) and DeepSeek-V3 kept it: the cache holds a compressed latent instead of per-head K and V. The up-projections are folded into the surrounding matmuls at inference time. That is a memory win, not a sparsity win: the attention still reads the compressed cache for every key it attends to.

<Callout type="key" title="Compression and sparsity are orthogonal">
MLA, GQA and FP8 KV change **how many bytes a token costs**. DSA and CSA/HCA change **how many tokens a query reads**. You can use both, and the strongest 2026 models do.
</Callout>

## Retrieve, then attend

**DeepSeek Sparse Attention** (DSA) adds a small **indexer** in front of attention. For each query, the indexer scores the cached keys cheaply and keeps the top *k* (typically a few thousand). The expensive QKᵀ and softmax then run only over that selected set.

The indexer does not make the scan disappear. It still looks at every key, which is the whole point: without scanning, you cannot know which keys matter. The saving comes from making each indexer score much cheaper than a full attention score. In practice the indexer is a small dot product over a compressed representation, and the selected blocks are attended at full precision.

At long context the indexer and Top-k selection become part of the critical path. NVIDIA's 2026 kernel analysis found the indexer plus Top-k taking **47% of a DeepSeek V3.2 prefill layer at 128K tokens**. That changes the optimisation target: fusing Top-k into the indexer, reusing the previous decode step's selection, and sharing selections across adjacent layers can matter as much as the attention kernel itself.

## Compress, then select: CSA and HCA

DeepSeek-V4 (April 2026) stacks the two ideas. Its **Compressed Sparse Attention** (CSA) first compresses the KV entries of every *m* tokens into one entry, then applies DSA over the compressed entries. **Heavily Compressed Attention** (HCA) compresses even more aggressively, with a much larger *m*. The two are interleaved across layers.

The reported result for DeepSeek-V4-Pro at 1M tokens is **27% of DeepSeek-V3.2's single-token inference FLOPs and 10% of its KV cache**. Those are vendor numbers on a specific model and workload, not a universal constant, but the direction is the point: compression makes the cache smaller, and sparsity makes the remaining attention cheaper.

The same pattern appears across the 2026 frontier: hybrid attention with a cheap selector, a compressed cache and a small full-attention window. The details differ by model; the serving questions do not.

## What this changes in the serving stack

Long context is a system property, not just a kernel choice.

- **Prefill** becomes the dominant TTFT term. [Chunked prefill](../chunked-prefill/) keeps decode streams alive while a long prompt is processed. [Context parallelism](../parallelism/) splits a prompt that does not fit on one device.
- **Decode** reads the KV cache on every step. Sparse attention reduces the selected reads; [KV offloading](../kv-offload/) can move the cold part of the cache out of HBM. Neither removes the capacity limit by itself.
- **Disaggregation** becomes more attractive: [prefill and decode](../disaggregation/) have different optimal parallelism when prompts are huge. The KV transfer is larger, so the network and the transfer engine matter more.
- **Routing and caching** matter more: a long prefix that is already cached is valuable. [Prefix caching](../prefix-caching/) and KV-aware routing should send a follow-up to the replica that already holds the prefix.
- **Capacity planning** must use the real context distribution, not the advertised maximum. A 1M-token window that is used by 1% of requests can still dominate the memory bill.

The [Inference Lab](../lab/) is the fastest way to see the trade-off: pick a model, raise the prompt length, and watch TTFT, KV memory and concurrency move together.

## How to evaluate a long-context stack

Do not accept a single "1M context" number. Measure the things your users will feel:

1. **Effective context**, not advertised context. Run retrieval and reasoning evals at the target length: RULER, LongBench, needle-in-a-haystack, and a task from your own domain. Accuracy often falls before the window does.
2. **TTFT and ITL at the target prompt and output lengths**, at p50, p95 and p99. Long prompts change the queueing behaviour, not just the kernel time.
3. **KV bytes per request** at the real context distribution, including the output tokens, and the concurrency that leaves after the weights and runtime overhead.
4. **The accuracy of the compressed or sparse path.** Turn the indexer or compression off and compare on your own evals; a small average-score drop can hide a large retrieval failure.
5. **The cost per successful request**, not tokens per second in isolation. A sparse model that is faster per token but needs more retries is not cheaper.

<Callout type="warn" title="Advertised context is not effective context">
A model can accept 1M tokens and still be poor at finding a fact at token 900,000. Sparse attention and compression are trained-in approximations; they change what the model can see. Validate on the task, at the length, before you put it in a capacity plan.
</Callout>

<Interview>
  <Q q="Why does prefill attention scale quadratically while decode attention scales linearly with context?" level="core">
    Prefill processes all prompt tokens at once, so every query attends to every key: n × n score pairs per layer. Decode processes one new token per step, so its one query attends to n cached keys: n score pairs, plus n KV bytes read. The quadratic term is paid once per prompt; the linear term is paid once per generated token.
  </Q>
  <Q q="Does sparse attention reduce KV cache memory? Explain the distinction." level="core">
    No. The cache still stores K and V (or the MLA latent) for every token, because a future query may select any of them. Sparse attention reduces the number of keys **read and attended to** per query, so it lowers attention compute and KV read traffic. Memory capacity falls only with compression (MLA, GQA, CSA/HCA) or lower-precision KV.
  </Q>
  <Q q="What does the indexer do in DSA, and why can it become the bottleneck?" level="mid">
    It scores every cached key with a cheap dot product, then keeps the top-k for full attention. The scan is deliberate: you cannot know which keys matter without looking. At long context the indexer touches the whole cache on every layer and step, and NVIDIA measured indexer plus Top-k at 47% of a DeepSeek V3.2 prefill layer at 128K. Fusing Top-k, reusing the previous selection and sharing selections across layers reduce that overhead.
  </Q>
  <Q q="How do CSA and HCA differ, and why interleave them?" level="mid">
    CSA compresses every m tokens into one KV entry and then applies sparse attention over the compressed entries. HCA compresses much more aggressively, with a larger m. Interleaving them keeps some layers at higher resolution and others at a much smaller cache, trading detail for memory and compute. The result is a much smaller cache and attention matrix at the cost of an approximation the model must be trained to tolerate.
  </Q>
  <Q q="A model vendor claims 1M-token context. What do you measure before capacity planning?" level="senior">
    Effective retrieval and reasoning accuracy at 1M on RULER, LongBench, needle tasks and a domain task; TTFT and ITL at the real prompt/output mix and at p95/p99; KV bytes per request at the output length; the concurrency left after weights and runtime overhead; and the quality delta with sparse attention or compression disabled. Then convert those into cost per successful request, not tokens per second alone.
  </Q>
  <Q q="When would you choose more compression versus more sparsity?" level="senior">
    Compress when HBM capacity or KV read bytes are the limit: more concurrency, longer contexts, or lower cost per request. Add sparsity when attention compute or KV read time dominates prefill/decode at long context and the model was trained for it. In practice use both, then validate the combination on the task because the errors are not additive in a simple way.
  </Q>
</Interview>
